# DREAM PARTY — ZOMBIE WEEK
## Working Concept Bible — Cat Rescue / The Floor Is Lava
**Status:** Latest working concept  
**Format:** AI Short Film  
**Target Length:** ~2:00–2:30 minutes (working target ~2:20)  
**Genre / Tone:** Survival Puzzle Comedy / Physical Comedy / Deadpan  
**Core Constraint:** Zombie outbreak world, but **do not focus on horror or zombie brutality**

---

# 1. Contest Brief — Relevant Constraints

Theme / core prompt:

> ตัวละครเอกต้องไปช่วยตัวละครอีก 1 ตัว ท่ามกลางซอมบี้ระบาด

Relevant rules:
- Short film length: **1–3 minutes**
- Protagonist can be ordinary or superpowered
- The character being rescued can be anyone / anything that can function as a character
- Zombie design is open-ended
- Tone can be horror, action, drama, comedy, etc.
- The story should be easy to understand, entertaining, emotionally engaging, and feel expandable
- AI tools/platforms are allowed

This concept interprets the rescue target as **a cat**.

---

# 2. Core Concept

A nerdy ordinary man has survived several days of a zombie outbreak by staying inside his apartment.

One day, while looking outside, he notices a cat casually walking along the exterior of the building opposite him — around balconies, ledges, air-conditioner compressors, or narrow building fixtures.

The cat itself is **not necessarily trapped**.

The real problem is that the cat is moving toward / around an apartment or area where a zombie is present.

The protagonist sees the danger before the cat does.

He decides to leave the safety of his room and cross the exterior gap between the buildings to intercept / rescue the cat.

But he cannot safely go down to street level because the ground below is filled with zombies.

So the entire mission becomes:

> **THE FLOOR IS LAVA — but the lava is zombies.**

The movie is about **HOW he gets there**, not about zombie mythology.

# 4. What Makes the Film Different

The film is **not** built around:
- zombie gore
- zombie brutality
- gunfights
- mass zombie action
- heroic combat
- infection lore
- “how scary are the zombies?”
- a skilled apocalypse survivor

The film is built around:

> **“How the hell is this guy going to cross 10–20 meters without touching the ground?”**

The zombies are not the dramatic center.

They function like:
- lava
- a penalty zone
- moving environmental hazards
- background NPCs
- a consequence if the protagonist falls or makes too much noise

The audience should spend more time watching:
- where his hands go
- where his feet go
- what can support his weight
- what object he can use next
- what almost breaks
- what unexpectedly works
- what fails at the worst possible moment

---

# 5. Protagonist — LOCKED DIRECTION

## Archetype
**Ordinary nerd who is completely out of his natural environment.**

He is NOT:
- a soldier
- a cop
- a parkour expert
- an action hero
- a prepper
- a survival specialist
- physically gifted
- “secretly badass”

He survives mostly because staying inside is something he is good at.

## Personality / Comic Engine
He is:
- intelligent enough to make plans
- analytical
- cautious
- somewhat over-prepared mentally
- under-prepared physically
- serious about every small problem
- not intentionally funny

Important:

> **He is not stupid. He simply has almost no real-world experience doing what he is attempting.**

His brain may understand the solution before his body is capable of executing it.

Examples:
- calculates a jump correctly → freezes when he sees the height
- understands weight distribution → legs shake while crossing
- carefully engineers a solution → forgot a simple practical issue
- knows he must stay silent → one piece of equipment rattles
- makes a reasonable plan → the building / cat / gravity ruins it

## Physical Comedy Rule
His courage should never look “cool”.

Avoid:
- heroic poses
- effortless jumps
- stylish parkour
- action-star landings

Prefer:
- hesitation
- awkward repositioning
- grabbing the wrong thing
- clumsy recovery
- relief after barely succeeding
- visible “why did I do this?” energy

---

# 6. Possible Nerd Inventory

Keep his tools domestic / nerdy rather than tactical.

Useful possibilities:
- duct tape
- cable ties
- screwdriver set
- multitool
- measuring tape / laser distance meter
- marker
- notebook
- power bank
- phone
- selfie stick
- cheap binoculars
- extension cord
- bedsheet
- belt
- mop / broom handle
- folding chair
- clothes hanger
- food can / tuna can

Avoid making him look apocalypse-ready:
- no tactical vest
- no large combat knife
- no professional climbing kit
- no grappling gun
- ideally no firearm as a normal solution

The fun comes from:
> **questionable home engineering used under real danger.**

---

# 7. The Cat — LOCKED DIRECTION

The cat should be a **normal cat**, not a deliberately “diva” or exaggerated comedy character.

Do NOT make it:
- intentionally arrogant
- knowingly mischievous
- cartoonishly smug
- obviously “playing a prank” on the protagonist
- aware that it is causing chaos

Instead, the cat should simply behave like a cat:
- walks wherever it wants
- stops randomly
- ignores calls
- changes direction without explanation
- sits down
- watches things
- cleans itself
- jumps easily
- may avoid being picked up
- may move at the worst possible time
- remains surprisingly calm

Comic contrast:

> **Protagonist = overthinking, planning, panic**  
> **Cat = walk, sit, jump, lick fur**

The cat is funny because it does **not know it is inside a rescue mission**.

---

# 8. Why the Cat Does NOT Need to Cry for Help

Latest preferred direction:

The protagonist does **not** necessarily hear a distressed cat crying.

He simply **sees it**.

Example:
- he looks out the window
- sees the cat walking on a balcony / ledge / AC compressor on the building opposite
- notices that the cat is approaching an open balcony / apartment where a zombie is wandering
- the cat is unaware of the danger
- the protagonist realizes what may happen

This motivation is stronger because:

> The cat never asked to be rescued.  
> The protagonist sees a danger and cannot ignore it.

---

# 9. Zombie Usage — VERY IMPORTANT

## Tone
**Do not showcase zombie horror.**

Avoid:
- gore close-ups
- grotesque zombie makeup as a focal point
- biting scenes
- blood splatter spectacle
- jump scares
- zombie faces screaming into camera
- “look how terrifying they are” shots

## Function
Zombies are environmental rules.

Main functions:

### 1. The ground is forbidden
If the protagonist could simply go downstairs, cross the street and go upstairs, the movie ends.

Zombie-filled street = **The Floor Is Lava**.

### 2. Noise has consequences
Dropping something may attract movement below.

But this can be played visually and comedically rather than frighteningly.

### 3. Zombies can become punchlines
Example:
- protagonist accidentally knocks a flower pot off a ledge
- it falls
- *bonk*
- zombie below collapses
- protagonist looks down
- pauses
- continues the mission

No gore required.

### 4. One zombie can function like a moving obstacle
If the cat is near an apartment with a zombie inside:
- the zombie may walk in a repetitive pattern
- the protagonist observes timing
- it becomes a stealth / timing puzzle
- the zombie is closer to an NPC hazard than a horror villain

---

# 10. Visual Concept — “THE FLOOR IS LAVA”

The geography must be extremely clear.

The audience should quickly understand:

- protagonist’s room
- opposite building
- cat position
- forbidden zombie-filled street below
- possible traversal elements between A and B

Possible traversal pieces:
- window ledge
- AC compressor
- drainage pipe
- balcony railing
- awning
- clothesline structure
- signboard
- narrow concrete ledge
- neighboring balcony
- external fire escape
- pipes
- improvised bridge

The movie should visually ask:

> **“Okay… how is he going to get from THERE to THERE?”**

Camera priorities:
- hands
- feet
- balance
- contact points
- failing screws
- bending metal
- slipping shoes
- loose knots
- distance between surfaces

Camera should care more about **route mechanics** than zombie faces.

---

# 11. Comedy Style — DEADPAN

Deadpan is strongly preferred.

Core rule:

> **Nobody knows they are in a comedy.**

The protagonist takes everything seriously.  
The zombies simply exist.  
The cat behaves normally.  
The world does not wink at the audience.

The comedy comes from:
- timing
- physical consequence
- mismatch between plan and reality
- absurdly small problems under huge stakes
- almost-failures
- accidental success
- the cat’s indifference

Avoid:
- comedy sound effects
- exaggerated reaction faces
- slapstick acting that breaks reality
- characters telling jokes
- dialogue designed as punchlines

---

# 12. “Almost Fucked Up” Comedy Engine

The desired feeling:

> **Every 10–15 seconds, something nearly ruins everything.**

But the protagonist somehow continues.

Ideal escalation:

1. simple crossing
2. slight instability
3. object fails
4. route changes
5. improvised solution
6. new unintended consequence
7. almost falls
8. succeeds by accident
9. immediately creates another problem

Important structural principle:

> **Whenever he solves one problem, the solution should create or reveal the next problem.**

This makes the movie feel continuous rather than like disconnected gag sketches.

---

# 13. Example Gags / Set Pieces

These are options, NOT all mandatory.

## Flower Pot
He carefully moves along a ledge.

Accidentally knocks a flower pot off.

He freezes.

Looks down.

*BONK.*

A zombie below drops.

Pause.

He looks at the empty spot where the flower pot used to be.

Continues.

---

## Safety Test
He taps possible footholds with a stick.

Tap.
Solid.

Tap.
Solid.

Tap.

Entire piece breaks off and falls.

He stares at the missing route.

Crosses that option out mentally / on his notes.

---

## Over-Engineering
He uses excessive duct tape to secure something.

Checks it carefully.

Looks satisfied.

Applies weight.

*CRACK.*

He looks at it.

Adds more duct tape.

---

## Silent Movement / Noisy Object
He carefully removes everything noisy from his body.

Takes one perfect step.

Something tiny still rattles.

He freezes.

---

## Cat Timing
He gets extremely close to the cat.

The cat casually walks to another ledge.

He has to redesign the route.

---

## Cat Is Better at Traversal
The protagonist spends ages engineering a bridge / route.

The cat looks at it.

Then simply jumps the gap naturally.

The protagonist stares.

---

## The Cat Cleans Itself
The protagonist is hanging in a dangerous position, struggling.

The cat sits nearby and starts grooming itself.

No acknowledgment.

---

## Accidental Zombie KO
An object falls because of the protagonist’s mistake and accidentally knocks out / kills a zombie below.

The protagonist did not intend it.

No gore.

No celebration.

Just a pause and continue.

# 14. Story Structure — CURRENT WORKING VERSION

Target runtime:
**~2:00–2:30**
Ideal working target:
**~2:20**

## 0:00–0:20 — Setup
- protagonist has clearly lived alone in this room for several days
- routine, calm, survival through staying indoors
- looks outside
- notices cat on the opposite building
- notices potential zombie danger nearby
- realizes the cat is walking toward trouble

No large exposition needed.

---

## 0:20–0:40 — Planning
He studies the gap between buildings.

Eyes trace possible route.

Possible visual:
- window
- AC compressor
- pipe
- awning
- ledge
- balcony
- cat

He gathers ridiculous household tools.

He believes he has a plan.

---

## 0:40–1:40 — Traversal / Main Film
The core “The Floor Is Lava” sequence.

The audience watches:
- attempt
- near failure
- recovery
- route adjustment
- accidental consequences
- physical comedy
- increasingly difficult movement

Zombies remain mostly below / peripheral.

This is the heart of the movie.

---

## ~1:40–2:05 — Near the Cat
He finally approaches the target.

Possible added tension:
- zombie inside nearby apartment is moving closer
- cat changes position
- protagonist cannot simply grab it
- route is unstable

The audience expects the rescue moment.

---

## ~2:05–2:20 — Payoff / Ending
Current preferred fucked-up ending direction:

Just as the protagonist finally reaches the cat / is about to rescue it…

**the cat casually jumps back toward where it originally came from / to safety by itself.**

The protagonist freezes.

The entire mission may have been unnecessary.

He is now stranded / exhausted / stuck in the more dangerous position.

The cat looks back at him.

Potential:
> “เมี้ยว”

Protagonist looks at cat.

Looks at the absurd route he crossed.

Looks down at zombies.

No big reaction.

**CUT TO BLACK.**

This ending is preferred over showing the full journey back.

---

# 15. Do We Need a Return Journey?

**No.**

Current preference:
Do **not** spend runtime on returning to his room.

Why:
- the main joke is stronger if the cat casually resolves the situation itself
- ending at the protagonist’s realization preserves the fucked-up deadpan tone
- a return journey may repeat the same traversal mechanic
- shorter ending gives a cleaner punchline

The movie can end with:

> Mission accomplished / mission unnecessary / protagonist now has his own problem.

---

# 16. Key Dramatic Irony

The audience and protagonist treat the situation as a serious rescue mission.

The cat does not.

Potential final realization:

> The protagonist risked his life solving a problem that might not have been a problem at all.

This should NOT make the protagonist look stupid.

He had a reasonable reason to believe the cat was in danger.

The humor comes from:
- the cat’s physical capability
- unpredictability
- the protagonist overcommitting to a rescue that the cat can partly solve itself

---

# 17. What the Movie Is Actually About

Not necessarily a deep thematic drama.

At its simplest, the film is:

> **A normal, slightly nerdy guy makes a terrible but compassionate decision to leave safety and help a cat — and spends the next two minutes narrowly surviving his own rescue plan.**

Optional underlying warmth:
- he could have ignored it
- helping the cat gives him a reason to leave isolation
- even in an apocalypse, people still do irrationally kind things

Do not over-explain this.

The comedy should remain dominant.

---

# 18. What NOT to Do

Avoid turning the concept into:

## Zombie Action
- no major gunfight
- no “hero kills 30 zombies”
- no action-star competence

## Horror Showcase
- no long zombie makeup shots
- no gore centerpiece
- no terror-first atmosphere

## Cartoon Comedy
- no exaggerated diva cat
- no talking cat
- no obvious gag music
- no broad slapstick performance

## Overcomplicated Lore
Do not explain:
- origin of outbreak
- government collapse
- infection rules
- zombie biology
- how many days the outbreak has lasted in exact detail

The viewer only needs enough context to understand:
**ground = unsafe.**

## Emotional Manipulation
No need for:
- dead family flashbacks
- child trauma
- melodramatic sacrifice
- “this cat belonged to my dead daughter” unless the concept later genuinely needs it

Current strength comes from simplicity.

# 20. Story Identity

The film should be remembered as:

> **“เรื่องเนิร์ดที่ปีนข้ามตึกไปช่วยแมวตอนซอมบี้ระบาด แล้วเกือบตายเพราะทุกอย่างพังตลอด”**

> “หนังซอมบี้อีกเรื่องหนึ่งที่มีแมว”

The signature mechanic is:

> **ZOMBIE APOCALYPSE AS THE FLOOR IS LAVA**

# 23. Next Development Step

The next useful task is **NOT writing dialogue**.

The next step should be to design the **physical geography of the entire sequence**:

1. Draw / define protagonist room
2. Define opposite building and cat path
3. Define zombie danger zones
4. Define 5–7 traversable objects
5. Build one continuous route
6. Decide where each “almost fucked up” beat occurs
7. Make every failure naturally create the next obstacle
8. End with the cat invalidating the entire rescue mission in one simple action

Once the geography works, the film can be storyboarded almost entirely visually.

---

# 24. Core Creative Rule to Protect

> **Do not ask “How do we make the zombies cooler?”**  
> **Ask “How do we make the next 2 meters harder, funnier, and more precarious?”**

That is the movie.
